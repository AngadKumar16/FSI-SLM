## Slide 1 — We ran real CFD on the held-out foil, and we know where it holds

**Figure (primary):** `generated/slide1_rans_validation.png`
**Figure (backup):** `generated/slide1_alt_convergence.png`

- 15 OpenFOAM cases on NACA 0009, k-ω SST turbulence, `simpleFoam`, 324 k prism
  cells, Re = 5 × 10⁵. The section is held out of training entirely.
- 7 of the 15 converged to the 10⁻⁴ residual tolerance. All of them sit at
  α ≤ 8°. Every case at α ≥ 10° ran into the 2,000-iteration cap with initial
  residuals around 10⁻², and lift oscillated by as much as ±0.43.
- On the converged branch the lift slope is 0.095 per degree, or 5.42 per
  radian, which is 86% of the 2π that thin-airfoil theory predicts. Minimum drag
  is 0.0078 at zero incidence against a published 0.006–0.008, and lift is
  symmetric about ±4° to within 2.3%.
- **We claim RANS validation for α ≤ 8° only.** The stalled cases are recorded
  with their residuals and excluded from every score.

### What the primary figure shows

Left panel is the lift curve. Filled teal points are the converged solves and
they land on a straight line that is slightly shallower than the dashed 2π
reference, which is what you expect for a real viscous 9%-thick section rather
than an idealised thin plate. The open red points past 10° carry error bars,
and those bars are not measurement noise: they are the standard deviation of Cl
over the last 500 SIMPLE iterations. A converged steady solution would have no
bar at all. The bars appearing exactly where the flow separates is the physical
story of the slide — steady RANS has no fixed point to find once the wake starts
shedding, so it limit-cycles instead.

Right panel is the drag bucket on a log axis. The minimum sits at α = 0 and the
curve is symmetric, both of which are required for a symmetric section, so this
is a cheap check that the mesh and the boundary conditions are not lying to us.

### What the backup figure shows

Same 15 cases, but it drops the physics and shows only the numerical health:
residual, lift oscillation, and iteration count, coloured by whether the case
passed. Use it if someone pushes on "how do you know which ones to throw away."
Three bar charts make the cutoff obvious — everything below 10° converges in
roughly 500 iterations at 10⁻⁵, everything at or above 10° hits the cap. There
is no judgement call in the middle.

### Likely question

*"Why keep the failed cases at all?"* Because throwing them away silently is how
you end up claiming a stall model you never validated. They are in the manifest
with a `converged: false` flag and a residual, and the eval script filters on it.

## Slide 2 — The surrogate was trained on panel data. We graded it against RANS.

**Figure (primary):** `generated/slide2_fidelity_transfer.png`
**Figure (backup):** `generated/slide2_alt_transfer_ratio.png`

- One checkpoint, `artifacts/sensor/run/best.pt`, 2.27 M parameters, epoch 58.
  It never saw a RANS case in training.
- Scored twice: against 293 panel-method cases on held-out sections, and against
  the 7 converged RANS cases on NACA 0009.
- Streamwise velocity error is *lower* against RANS (0.020) than against the
  panel targets it was trained on (0.025). Drag MAE is the same to four decimals,
  0.0029 either way.
- Lift error grows 2.4×, from 0.033 to 0.078. The panel backend runs 5–15% high
  near stall by construction, and the network learned that bias along with the
  physics.
- Structural channels do not survive the switch. Displacement goes from 0.239 to
  0.521 relative RMSE, and load from 0.040 to 0.126.

### What the primary figure shows

Left panel pairs the two scores channel by channel. The teal bar is the grade
against panel targets, the coral bar against RANS. Read it as: how much of what
this network learned is about fluid mechanics, and how much is about the quirks
of the solver that generated its labels. If the network had memorised panel
artefacts, every coral bar would be much longer than its teal partner. Instead
`u` gets shorter, which is the strongest single piece of evidence we have that
the surrogate is doing physics.

Right panel isolates the integrated forces on a log axis, because Cl and Cd MAE
differ by an order of magnitude and would be unreadable on a shared linear
scale. Drag is identical across fidelities. Lift is not, and the reason is
specific and fixable: drag comes mostly from the wake momentum deficit, which
both backends model consistently, while lift comes from the leading-edge
pressure peak, which is exactly where a panel method and a RANS solve disagree.

### What the backup figure shows

The same data collapsed into one number per quantity: the ratio of RANS error to
panel error. A dot to the left of the dashed line means the quantity got
*better* under the harder grader. This version is easier to read from the back of
a room and it makes the ranking explicit, but it hides the absolute error, so a
quantity can look bad by ratio while still being accurate in absolute terms.

Note the two figures rank the failures differently and both rankings are true.
By ratio, `load` is worst (×3.13). By absolute error, `disp` is worst (0.521).
`load` starts from a very low panel-side error of 0.040, so tripling it still
leaves it below the pressure field. If you show the backup, say which one you
mean.

### The honest read

Velocity and drag transfer across the fidelity gap. Lift carries a known,
systematic, correctable bias. The structural channels need RANS cases in
*training*, not just in test, because the chordwise beam integrates the
leading-edge pressure difference and amplifies exactly the disagreement between
the two solvers. That is the next run: regenerate a RANS subset through the same
tensor contract and fine-tune the panel-pretrained network on it.

### Likely questions

*"Seven cases is a small test set."* Yes. It is enough to establish direction and
sign, not a precise error bar. The panel-side numbers carry n = 293.

*"Why not just train on RANS from the start?"* A RANS case takes about four
minutes; the panel backend takes under a second. 1,913 panel cases cost less
than an hour, and the transfer result says most of that pretraining survives.

## One inconsistency to fix before you present

`docs/results.md` §4 reports the lift slope as 0.099 per degree, or 90% of 2π.
Fitting all seven converged points gives 0.095 per degree, 86% of 2π. The
figure uses 0.095 because the script fits the whole converged branch, −8° to 8°.
The 0.099 figure comes out only from a narrower fit near zero incidence
(−4° to 4° gives 0.0978). Both are defensible; they should not be different
numbers in two places. Pick one range, state it, and update the other file.
